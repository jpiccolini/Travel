Le quartier Avenue du Drapeau — where it sits

Avenue du Drapeau runs north out of place de la République, and for most of the twentieth century a good stretch of it was army buildings. The Junot and Heudelet barracks sat along here until the city took them back.

When Dijon built its tram in 2012, the T2 line was routed straight up this corridor — and the disused military ground either side of it became the obvious place to put new housing. Whole blocks of flats, shops and public buildings went up on the old parade grounds, deliberately clustered around the tram stops. The building in these photographs is one of them: red brick and pale render, shops on the ground floor, a rooftop terrace at the top.

It’s an easy piece of city to live in. République — where the T1 and T2 lines cross, and where you change for anywhere — is a few minutes’ walk south. The square is the old Saint-Nicolas parade ground, and the Sadi Carnot monument at the centre of it got its ring of water jets in 2012, part of the same tram works that remade the avenue. There’s an Intermarché a short way up, a bigger one down at the République end, and the medieval centre is about twenty-five minutes on foot or three stops on the tram.

  • TramT2 (Valmy ↔ Chenôve) runs the length of the avenue; T1 and T2 meet at République
  • Fare≈ €1.60 single, valid one hour with transfers; ≈ €4.50 for 24 hours. Contactless card works at the validator
  • To the centre~1.7 km south to the Palais des Ducs — walkable, flat
  • To the stationGare Dijon-Ville, ~2 km southwest; Porte-Neuve (regional) is closer

We found this town a manageable size, about 160,000 people, right in Bourgogne (Burgundy), wine country.

After a search in 2025 in Le Mans together, and by Jim in Dijon and Strasbourg, we decided to come back together to Dijon. We are overjoyed to have family nearby here, and the town is walkable and pleasant. With a tram and bus system, a university and lots of great places to walk around and eat, we love it.

Dijon & la Bourgogne The city and the region

Dijon is the capital of Bourgogne-Franche-Comté, a city of roughly 160,000 in a metropolitan area of about 250,000 — big enough to have everything, small enough to cross on foot.

A capital before it was a provincial city

It began as Divio, a Roman garrison on the road from Lyon to Mainz. In 1015 Duke Robert made it the seat of the Duchy of Burgundy, and in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the Valois dukes — Philip the Bold through Charles the Bold — ran a state that stretched from the Alps to the North Sea and was, for a while, richer than the French crown. They spent that money here. The Palais des Ducs et des États is still the centre of town: the city council sits in the middle of it, the Musée des Beaux-Arts occupies the east wing, and the tombs of the dukes are in what used to be the guards’ room.

The protected historic quarter covers about 97 hectares and is almost entirely pedestrianised — half-timbered houses, Romanesque and Gothic churches, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century hôtels particuliers. Most of the municipal museums are free.

Two UNESCO listings, and they’re connected

In 2015 UNESCO inscribed Les Climats du vignoble de Bourgogne — the 1,247 precisely-drawn vineyard parcels running south from Dijon along the Côte de Nuits and the Côte de Beaune. The listing deliberately includes the historic centre of Dijon itself, because this is where the legal and political machinery that defined those parcels lived. Beaune was the trade; Dijon was the rule-making.

Separately, the gastronomic meal of the French was added to UNESCO’s intangible heritage list in 2010, and Dijon was chosen to host the wine-facing part of the national response. The Cité Internationale de la Gastronomie et du Vin opened in 2022 on the site of the general hospital that ran from 1204 to 2015 — a €250m conversion with exhibitions, a Ferrandi cooking school, a very large cellar, and a new residential quarter around it.

What it tastes like

Mustard, obviously — the Dijon method grinds the seed with verjuice or white wine rather than vinegar. Beyond that: pain d’épices, crème de cassis (and therefore kir), Époisses, jambon persillé, œufs en meurette, boeuf bourguignon. Les Halles, the market hall built to a Gustave Eiffel design, trades on Tuesday, Friday and Saturday mornings.

Worth knowing before you plan a day

A lot of provincial France closes on Sunday and Monday, and Dijon is no exception — check before you build an afternoon around a particular shop or restaurant. The Musée des Beaux-Arts is closed Tuesdays. And the Parcours de la Chouette, the owl trail, is a genuinely good way to get your bearings on a first walk: 22 brass plaques set into the pavement, following the route between the monuments. The owl itself is carved into a buttress of Notre-Dame; you touch it with your left hand.

Y aller Getting there

Dijon-Ville sits on the old Paris–Lyon–Marseille main line, 314 km from Paris-Lyon, and it has been a junction since 1849. Eight million passengers a year pass through it. Coming from the United States, the sane route is a flight into Paris and the TGV out of Gare de Lyon.

Once you’re there

The Divia network is two tram lines and a set of bus routes, and it will get you anywhere you need. T1 runs east–west from the station through the centre to Quetigny; T2 runs north–south from Valmy down to Chenôve, up avenue du Drapeau and through République. Tap a contactless card on the validator every time you board, including transfers. A single is about €1.60 and lasts an hour; a 24-hour pass is about €4.50 and a week is around €15. There’s bike share, and a free shuttle around the historic centre.

From Gare Dijon-Ville the T1 puts you in the centre in two stops, but it’s a flat ten-minute walk and the walk is nicer.

We have traveled to and from here via Charles DeGaulle airport in Paris, but that is so hectic that we have since tried traveling via Lyon, which is much easier to navigate, and is not a long TGV ride away.
